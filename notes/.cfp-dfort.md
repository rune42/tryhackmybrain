# DFort Systems — Ransomware Incident: Three-Act Security Talk

**Working concept**: A one-person performance combining a fictional ransomware incident with technical commentary.
---
**Important framing**: DFort Systems is fictional. The attack chain is based on publicly documented observations, primarily Huntress' investigation of CitrixBleed 2 incidents. Do not present DFort Systems as an actual victim.
---
## Cast
### Systems Architect — "Architect"
- Played as myself.
- Technical, confident, occasionally overconfident.
- Explains the architecture and attack chain between scenes.
- Represents the assumption that security controls work as designed.
### Attacker / "Pentester"
- Black baseball cap and laptop prop.
- Technically competent but theatrical.
- Claims to be performing an authorized assessment while behaving like an attacker.
- Represents the attacker's view of the organization's actual attack surface.
- Keep demonstrations simulated; no real ransomware deployment or operational exploitation.
### CISO
- Blue blazer over T-shirt.
- Upright, corporate, concerned with risk and business impact.
- Represents management, incident response, budget, and accountability.

## ACT I — ENTRY
###  Scene 1: Normal operations

#### Stage

> Architect presents DFort Systems as an ordinary organization.
"We're not a particularly unusual company. We've got employees, Windows endpoints, Active Directory, remote access, some cloud services, and the usual collection of things that somehow became critical infrastructure."

> Introduce the remote-access infrastructure:
- Citrix NetScaler Gateway / AAA
- LDAP authentication
- MFA / OTP
- Windows domain environment
- Administrative remote-access tools

Slide: "The Perimeter"

Show:

Internet
↓
Citrix NetScaler
├── LDAP authentication
├── MFA / OTP
└── Internal environment
    ├── Active Directory
    ├── Workstations
    └── Servers

Scene 2: "We have MFA"

CISO enters.

CISO:

"We're protected. We have MFA."

Architect agrees.

"Yes. Authentication requires credentials plus a second factor."

Attacker enters wearing the black cap.

"Cool."

Pause.

"What version is your NetScaler?"

Slide: "CVE-2025-5777 — CitrixBleed 2"

Explain:

CVE-2025-5777 is a critical Citrix NetScaler vulnerability.
It involves a pre-authentication memory-read condition.
An attacker can potentially obtain sensitive information from NetScaler memory.
The important consequence for this case study is exposure of authentication/session material.

Do not portray this as "MFA is cracked."

Scene 3: The session

Show the authentication flow:

Employee
↓
username + password
↓
NetScaler
↓
MFA / OTP
↓
Authenticated session

Then introduce the attacker:

Attacker
↓
abuses vulnerable NetScaler
↓
NetScaler memory
↓
session material

Architect:

"The employee really did authenticate."

CISO:

"Then MFA worked?"

Architect:

"Yes."

Pause.

"That's the problem."

Explain the distinction:

MFA authenticates the user.
The resulting session is then trusted by the application.
If an attacker steals a valid session, they may not need to perform authentication themselves.
This is fundamentally different from defeating the second factor cryptographically.
Slide: "MFA was not bypassed in the conventional sense"

Key message:

An attacker can attack the authenticated session rather than the authentication ceremony.

ACT II — THE ATTACK CHAIN
Scene 4: From session to system

Remove the comedy for a moment.

Slide: "The trust boundary moved"

Show:

Initial access
↓
Stolen authenticated session
↓
Internal Windows environment
↓
Privilege escalation
↓
Persistence

Explain that Huntress observed attackers progressing from hijacked sessions into Windows environments.

Mention observed techniques at a high level:

Privilege escalation
Rogue administrator accounts
RDP
PsExec
Credential-access tooling
Remote-management software

Avoid operational exploit instructions.

Scene 5: Legitimate tools become suspicious

Attacker starts "administrating" the environment.

Architect:

"There's nothing particularly exotic here. That's what makes this uncomfortable."

Slide: "Living on the legitimate side of the fence"

Mention examples reported by Huntress:

ScreenConnect
Zoho Assist
RDP
PsExec

Explain:

"The attacker doesn't necessarily need a movie-villain malware binary. Legitimate administrative tooling can provide persistence and remote control while blending into normal IT activity."

CISO:

"So our monitoring sees administrators doing administrator things?"

Architect:

"Exactly."

Scene 6: Detection opportunity
Slide: "Where could we have stopped this?"

Potential intervention points:

Patch the vulnerable NetScaler.
Detect anomalous session use.
Detect suspicious changes in session origin.
Monitor creation of unexpected privileged accounts.
Detect unusual remote-management tooling.
Monitor lateral movement.
Protect and isolate critical administrative infrastructure.
Maintain recoverable, isolated backups.

Important transition:

This isn't about finding one magical security control. The objective is to create multiple opportunities to break the attack chain.

ACT III — IMPACT
Scene 7: The final stage

The attacker stops typing.

Silence.

CISO receives notifications.

Slide: "Encryption"

Show:

Initial access
↓
Session hijacking
↓
Privilege escalation
↓
Persistence
↓
Lateral movement
↓
Credential access
↓
Ransomware deployment
↓
Business impact

Explain that Huntress reported one investigated CitrixBleed 2 case progressing to DragonForce ransomware encryption.

For the dramatization, do not claim that DFort Systems itself was the real victim.

Scene 8: The business impact

Do not focus only on the ransom note.

Instead, show:

IT: systems unavailable
Finance: ERP / financial workflows disrupted
Operations: critical systems unavailable
Legal: incident and notification questions
Security: containment and investigation
Executive team: "How did this happen?"

CISO:

"How much did the ransomware cost us?"

Architect:

"That's the wrong first question."

Pause.

"The first question is how many opportunities we had to stop it."

FINAL SCENE — THE LESSON

All three characters return.

The CISO

"We had MFA."

The Architect

"Yes."

The Attacker

"I didn't need your MFA."

The CISO

"We had endpoint protection."

The Architect

"Yes."

The Attacker

"I used your administration tools."

The CISO

"So what actually failed?"

Architect addresses audience.

"Not one thing."

"A vulnerability exposed an authentication boundary. A valid session became attacker-controlled. That access became privilege. Privilege became persistence. Persistence became lateral movement. And eventually, an attacker reached the systems that mattered to the business."

Final slide
Break the chain.

Security isn't about having one impressive control.

It's about having enough independent opportunities to stop the attacker before the final stage.

TECHNICAL TAKEAWAYS FOR THE ACCOMPANYING SLIDES
1. MFA

Avoid:

"MFA doesn't work."

Prefer:

"MFA protects authentication. It does not automatically protect every authenticated session."

Discuss:

Authentication vs. session security
Phishing-resistant MFA
Session theft
Conditional access
Device and session monitoring
2. Internet-facing infrastructure

Discuss:

Asset inventory
Patch management
Exposure management
Emergency patching for actively exploited vulnerabilities
Monitoring of externally accessible authentication infrastructure
3. Identity

Discuss:

Privileged accounts
Session anomalies
Account creation
Credential theft
Administrative authentication paths
Separation of administrative identities
4. Lateral movement

Discuss:

RDP
PsExec
Remote-management software
Administrative shares
Segmentation
Least privilege
5. Detection

Ask:

"Would our SOC recognize this as an attack?"

Potential signals:

A session appearing from an unexpected network origin
New privileged accounts
Unusual remote-management software
Unexpected administrative activity
Abnormal authentication patterns
Credential-access behavior
Sudden large-scale file modification
6. Recovery

Discuss:

Offline / isolated backups
Tested restoration
Recovery priorities
Identity recovery
Domain recovery planning
Business continuity
